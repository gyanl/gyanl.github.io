I joined [Public Knowledge Studio](https://www.publicknowledge.co) as a founding partner in January 2024. Two years and eight months later, I've had a hand in twenty-two of the twenty-five projects we've published, and I've spent more time than I expected thinking about things that have nothing to do with design — invoicing, hiring, whether we could afford another intern, how to tell a client no.

This is a look back at that.

### What the studio is

The line we settled on is that Public Knowledge is a design studio that makes technology work for humans. That sounds like the kind of thing every studio says, and in fairness it mostly is. What it meant in practice was narrower and more useful: we took on brand identity, interface and experience design, front-end development, and product strategy, and we tried very hard not to hand any of them off to each other as separate jobs.

That last part is the actual position. Plenty of studios will do your brand and then wish you luck with the build. We kept development in-house specifically so that the thing we designed and the thing that shipped were the same thing. It is a less efficient way to run a studio. It is a much better way to make something you'd want to put your name on.

### The work

The range is the part I still find funny. In two and a half years we worked on a website for a company with a patented process for turning plastic waste into fuel, an e-commerce flow for a bakery that makes fully custom cakes, and the identity for sā Biennale, a land art festival held at altitude in the Himalayas. Somewhere in the same stretch: branding and product design for SCC Online, India's leading law publisher, and landing pages for [Sarvam](https://www.sarvam.ai/), building sovereign AI infrastructure for India.

A cakery and a sovereign AI platform. I don't think I could have designed that portfolio on purpose.

The AI work ended up being a real throughline — Sycamore, Memory Store, ressl.ai, Neels.ai, Autotelic, Presentations.ai, Mercury for lawyers, Listen for emotional-AI products. A lot of these companies came to us with genuinely novel technology and no idea how to say what it did. That turns out to be the job more often than not: not making the thing beautiful, but making it legible. The AHFL project was the clearest version of it. A chemical breakthrough is worth nothing commercially if the people who need to fund it can't follow the sentence explaining it.

The smaller projects taught me more than the prestigious ones, which is an annoying thing to have to admit. Bobo Cakery was a bakery website, and the interesting problem wasn't visual at all — it was working out that low-value orders should go through the site and high-value custom ones should go to a WhatsApp conversation with a human, because the team's bandwidth was the actual constraint. Nobody's putting that flowchart in an award submission. It was the right answer.

### The people

I want to be specific here, because "and the team was great" is the emptiest sentence in this genre.

[Charu Pragya](https://www.publicknowledge.co/charu) is the other founding partner, and building something with one other person for two and a half years is a particular kind of relationship — closer to a band than a company. Most of what I know about running a studio I worked out in conversation with her, usually while disagreeing.

Saurabh Yadav led product design. Anandu Gopal covered product and web, Namita Sunil branding and web design, Sharang Sharma brand identities, Ravee Deshpande UX research and branding, Mohit Jakhar motion and video, Rakesh Rawat development. Harshit Chandna, Devina Narang, Raina Motihar, Ananya Mehra and Devika Kohli came through as interns, and several of the projects above have their fingerprints on them in ways the credits don't fully capture.

Being small means every hire is a bet you can feel. I ended up reviewing a lot of portfolios — enough that I [wrote a whole post about it](https://gyanl.com/how-to-build-a-ux-design-portfolio) — and the thing that surprised me was how many genuinely good designers were losing out to bad presentation of good work. On the other side of the table, you learn quickly that you are not hiring a skill set. You're hiring someone you'll be in a room with on a bad Thursday.

### What I'd tell myself in January 2024

**Client work is a filtering problem, not a sales problem.** The projects that went badly were almost never badly executed. They were badly chosen — a mismatch of expectations we could have caught in the first conversation if we'd been willing to ask the uncomfortable question. Learning to say no is a skill with a direct line to how much you enjoy your job.

**Keeping the build in-house is worth what it costs.** It slowed us down and it made hiring harder. It also meant we shipped things that matched the design, which sounds like a low bar until you've watched it fail.

**Scope everything twice.** Once for what the client asked for, once for what they actually want. These are different documents and the gap between them is where studios lose money.

**The work compounds and the anxiety doesn't.** Every quiet month felt like the beginning of the end. None of them were. I wish I'd spent less of two and a half years bracing.

**Write things down.** Half of what we learned lived in two people's heads for far too long. The studio site, the project write-ups, the service descriptions — all of that started as an attempt to stop re-deriving our own opinions every quarter.

### And

Twenty-two projects, fourteen-odd people, a fuel company and a cakery and a biennale in the mountains. It's a strange list and I'm fond of all of it.

Thanks to everyone who worked on it, and to the clients who let us.
